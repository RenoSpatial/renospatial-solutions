Use Cases

Problems worth automating

Most organizations lose hours every week to work a computer should be doing. These are the patterns that come up again and again — and what modern automation, AI and GIS actually do about them.

Manual data entry and re-keying

The problemStaff type the same information into several systems — a permit into a spreadsheet, an invoice into finance, field notes into the GIS. It is slow, it introduces typos, and the versions drift apart.

What solves itAutomation moves each record between your systems the moment it is created, so information is entered once and appears everywhere it is needed. Usually built with Microsoft Power Automate — or whichever automation tool best fits the software you already run.

Data locked inside documents

The problemCritical information sits in PDFs, scanned drawings, emailed forms and attachments that nobody has time to transcribe — so it never reaches a database where it can be reported on.

What solves itAI reads those documents much as a person would — picking out the dates, amounts, names and addresses. The results are checked automatically for errors, then filed straight into your system. Nobody retypes anything. Built with AI document processing alongside data tools such as FME.

Sorting, categorizing and routing

The problemService requests, emails and reports are triaged by hand. It is inconsistent, it slows response times, and the knowledge often lives with one person.

What solves itAI classification tags each item and routes it to the right team automatically, using rules and confidence thresholds you control — with anything uncertain still going to a human.

Reporting and communication silos

The problemEach team keeps its own version of the truth in disconnected systems and private spreadsheets, and nobody is confident which number is current. Status travels by email chains, chased phone calls and standing meetings that exist only because there is nowhere to look it up.

What solves itConnecting the systems you already run so they share one agreed set of records, with shared dashboards everyone can open and updates that arrive on their own. Routine reports are generated and distributed automatically instead of being assembled and emailed by hand. Built with integration tools such as Power Platform, FME or open-source alternatives, whichever suits your setup.

Technology you already pay for, unused

The problemMicrosoft 365, Power Platform and ArcGIS licences routinely include capability that was never switched on. Organizations buy new tools to solve problems their existing stack already covers.

What solves itAn assessment of what you already own, then a plan to activate it. In most engagements the first wins cost nothing in new licensing.

Not knowing what is cost-effective to adopt

The problemThe vendor landscape is overwhelming and every product claims to do everything. It is hard for a small team to judge what genuinely fits their budget and capacity.

What solves itVendor-neutral advice on right-sized tools, with honest estimates of licence cost, integration effort and how long it takes to pay back.

Legacy systems that should be retired

The problemPaper forms still being filled in by hand. A spreadsheet doing the job of a database. Software that lost vendor support years ago, or a product whose vendor has refused to modernize for your needs and offers no way to integrate or automate. That is usually the clearest sign a system has outlived its usefulness — it quietly caps what everyone around it can do.

What solves itSometimes replacement is the honest answer. When a system cannot integrate, cannot automate and cannot be supported, the cost of working around it has already passed the cost of moving on. I help you make that call with evidence, plan the migration, carry the data across intact, and choose a successor that will still be maintainable in five years.

AI Adoption

An AI agent that does the work

An AI agent that cannot reach your systems can only offer general advice. What makes one genuinely useful is everything you connect to it: secure access to the software you already run, skills built for the jobs your organization actually does, and orchestration so agents, skills and automations can hand work to one another. Built properly it becomes a shared workspace that grows with you — where most of the day's work gets finished without leaving it.

An agent that can see your workplace

TodayAnswers live scattered across Outlook, Teams, SharePoint or Gmail, Chat and Drive. Finding what was agreed with a client three months ago means searching three applications and hoping you recall the right keyword.

ConnectedThe agent is given secure access to Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace so it can read across email, chat and files with your existing permissions intact. Ask a plain question, get an answer assembled from the actual threads and documents.

Chat with your business data

TodayGetting a number out of your everyday systems — the ticket system, customer records, finance, the asset register — means requesting a report, waiting for the one person who can write it, and receiving a spreadsheet that is already out of date.

ConnectedThe agent is connected directly to those systems, so staff simply ask a question and get an answer from live information. No technical skills needed, no report queue, no waiting.

Draft and resolve, not just search

TodayEvery service ticket is read, researched and answered from scratch, even when it is the same question your team answered last week.

ConnectedThe agent drafts the reply using that customer's history and your knowledge base, ready for a human to check and send. For routine issues it can guide staff — or the customer — to resolution by chat or voice, and escalate anything it is not confident about.

See your clients and work on a map

TodayCustomer and job information sits in rows on a screen. Things that would be obvious on a map — where your work clusters, how far crews travel, which areas you barely cover, where demand is growing — stay hidden in a spreadsheet.

ConnectedMapping puts clients, sites and jobs on a map anyone in the office can open. This one is optional — plenty of businesses never need it. If you do, it can be built with free open-source mapping, with Esri's ArcGIS, or with the drag-and-drop mapping already bundled in licences you hold. What matters is that it keeps working and stays easy to maintain after I hand it over.

Skills and orchestration that compound

TodayEvery tool is a separate destination. Staff jump between a dozen applications to finish one task, carrying context in their heads from one to the next, and any AI in the mix is a side trip rather than part of the work.

ConnectedBeyond connecting systems, an agent can be given skills — repeatable procedures for the specific jobs your organization does, written once and reused. Then orchestration lets agents, skills and existing automations hand work to one another, so a single request can pull the data, do the analysis, draft the output and update the records. It becomes a shared working environment that grows as you add skills, and the aim is simple: finish as much of the day inside it as possible instead of touring your software to get one thing done.

Dashboards, reports and project visibility

TodaySomeone spends the first week of every month assembling a report by hand. Project status lives partly in a spreadsheet, partly in an inbox, and partly in one person's memory. By the time the figures are circulated they are old, and the decision has already been made without them.

ConnectedLive dashboards show what is actually happening — workload, response times, costs, which services are growing and where. Projects and tasks sit in one place the whole team can see, so nobody chases updates. Routine reports build and send themselves on a schedule, and the agent can summarise the trend in plain English instead of leaving you to read the chart.

FAQ

Common questions, eh?

What kinds of work can Power Automate actually automate?

Far more than shuffling spreadsheet rows. Automation can watch the places work actually arrives — Outlook and shared mailboxes, voicemail and phone systems, Teams or Slack messages, web forms, scanned paperwork — and turn that messy incoming traffic into structured data. From there it triggers whatever should happen next: pull out the key details, categorize the request, assign it to the right person or team, raise the task or ticket, notify whoever needs to know, update the other systems that hold the same record, and escalate anything that misses a deadline. If someone is following the same steps every time, it is usually a candidate.

How does AI help with manual data entry and extraction?

AI document processing reads unstructured files — PDFs, scanned forms, invoices, emailed attachments — and returns structured fields you can store and query. Combined with an automated flow, a document that arrives by email can be read, validated and written into a database without anyone retyping it.

Do we have to replace our existing systems?

It genuinely depends, and it is a decision we make together. Often the answer is no — connecting what you already run is faster, more affordable and far less disruptive than replacing it, and it protects the investment you have already made. But sometimes a system really is holding you back. In that case I will tell you plainly which software or systems I would recommend replacing or migrating and why, then work through the evaluation with you — weighing cost, risk, disruption and what your team can realistically support. You make the final call with a clear picture rather than a sales pitch.

Can automation work with our existing GIS?

One hundred percent. ArcGIS Enterprise and ArcGIS Online both provide connections that automation can read from and write to. More importantly, tools like FME and Python let me work with far more than the map layer — I can pull attribute data from multiple systems and databases, join it to your spatial records, check it for errors, and push the results back out to wherever they are needed. Common examples include keeping asset layers in step with a maintenance system, or plotting incoming service requests on a map automatically.

What is MCP, and why does it matter for AI at work?

MCP (Model Context Protocol) is an open standard for connecting an AI agent to real systems and data, giving it controlled, permission-aware access to Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, ticketing systems, CRMs and databases. But MCP is only one piece. An agent also needs skills — repeatable procedures for the specific jobs your organization does — and orchestration, so agents, skills and your existing automations can pass work between them. Together those turn a general-purpose assistant into a working environment that grows with your business over time.

Is it safe to let AI read our email and business systems?

It can be, when access is scoped deliberately. Connections are configured to respect the permissions each user already has, limited to the systems you approve, and set to read-only wherever writing is not required. Actions that send or change something are kept behind human review. The right design question is not whether AI is allowed in, but exactly what it may see and what it may do.

Do you only work with Microsoft and Esri products?

No. Those come up often because so many organizations already pay for them, so using them costs nothing extra. But the right tool depends on what you already run and what you are trying to fix. I work with other automation platforms, other databases, open-source components and non-Esri mapping where those are the better fit. The goal is the outcome you need at a sensible cost, not loyalty to a particular vendor.

Is mapping or GIS required to work with you?

Not at all. Many projects are pure automation and integration with no map anywhere in sight. Mapping is offered because it is genuinely useful when your work has a geographic pattern — customers spread across a region, crews travelling between sites, assets in the field. If that is not your situation, we simply skip it.

When is it better to replace a system than automate around it?

When the system cannot be integrated, cannot be automated, and can no longer be supported. Clear signs include paper forms still being filled in by hand, a spreadsheet doing a database's job, software past its end of support, or a vendor who has refused for years to modernize or provide any way to connect. At that point the cost of working around it has usually already exceeded the cost of replacing it — and I will tell you when I think that line has been crossed.

Should we use open-source or the enterprise tools we already license?

Whichever is more affordable to own over the long term — and that is about much more than the sticker price. If your ArcGIS or Power Platform licences already cover the need, using them is usually the better answer: no new spend, no new vendor, and your staff may already know the tools. Where they do not fit, open-source components can do the job without a recurring fee. The deciding factors are risk, maintenance and continuity — who can support it in three years, how easily your own team can take it over, and what happens if a vendor changes direction or an open-source project loses its maintainers. I weigh those with you project by project rather than defaulting to a favourite.

How much does workflow automation cost for a small organization?

There are two different kinds of cost, and it helps to separate them. Automation and integration that does not involve AI can usually be scoped to a fixed price — the build is largely a one-off, and much of the capability may already be bundled in Microsoft 365 or Power Platform licences you hold, so the starting point is often lower than expected. AI is different. When a workflow sends documents or questions through an AI service, you pay for what you consume, more like a utility bill for water or electricity than a flat subscription. That is predictable once we measure your actual volumes, and I will estimate it before you commit rather than after the first invoice arrives.
